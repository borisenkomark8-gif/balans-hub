Metabolism

Type 2 diabetes

The goal is not only to lower a single glucose number but to reduce long-term complication risk and preserve quality of life. Nutrition and training work together with medicines, monitoring, and individual targets.

Where to start Nutrition Physical activity Safety Monitoring When urgent care is needed

01 · Baseline

Where to start

Goals

Set them individually

HbA1c and glucose targets depend on age, diabetes duration, hypoglycemia risk, comorbidities, and treatment.

Risks

Look beyond glucose

Control blood pressure and lipids, assess kidney function, eyes and feet, and overall cardiovascular risk.

Treatment

Know how medicines work

Food and activity change glucose levels. Do not adjust drug doses on your own — plans are agreed with your clinician.

Trends

Look for patterns

A single reading rarely explains the picture. It helps to compare meals, activity, medicines, sleep, and glucose over several days.

02 · Nutrition

Structure matters more than bans

Build a predictable diet

  • Vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and other fiber sources.
  • Enough protein for kidney function.
  • Mostly unsaturated fats; less trans fat and excess saturated fat.
  • Minimal sugary drinks and fewer highly processed products.

Work with carbohydrates

Carbohydrates do not have to be eliminated. Amount, quality, pairing with protein and fiber, and individual glucose response all matter.

If overweight, a moderate sustained calorie reduction is usually better than a harsh short diet. Eating pattern must fit medicines and hypoglycemia risk.

Simple meal guide: a large share of the plate — non-starchy vegetables, then a protein source and a controlled portion of starchy side. This is not a universal medical plan, but a practical starting point.

03 · Movement

Combine different types of load

Aerobic work150 minmoderate intensity per week as a general reference
Strength training2–3 daysper week if no restrictions
SittingBreak it upwith short movement periods through the day

Aerobic load

Walking, cycling, swimming, and other accessible options improve tolerance and help manage cardiovascular risk. Start with a doable volume.

Strength load

Training major muscle groups helps preserve strength and mass. Increase volume and intensity gradually based on symptoms and diabetes complications.

04 · Safety

Check before exercise

Insulin and hypoglycemia-risk medicines. Agree in advance when to check glucose, how to account for food, and what to do if levels drop. Have fast-acting carbohydrate available during exercise.

SGLT2 inhibitors. Illness, dehydration, prolonged fasting, and very low-carb eating can raise ketoacidosis risk. You need a separate sick-day and perioperative plan.

Feet, vision, heart, and kidneys. Neuropathy, foot ulcers, significant retinopathy, heart disease, and kidney disease require adapted exercise type and intensity.

How you feel. Do not start intense training during acute illness, marked weakness, or unexplained high or low glucose.

If glucose is below 3.9 mmol/L and the person is conscious: usually take 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate and recheck in 15 minutes. Repeat if hypoglycemia persists. Individual plans take priority.

05 · Follow-up

What to track

Glucose. How often you measure depends on treatment and goals. Note context: meal, exercise, medicine, and symptoms.

HbA1c and check-ups. Timing is set by your care team based on stability and treatment changes.

Weight, waist, and activity. Assess trends over weeks, not daily swings.

When urgent care is needed

Severe hypoglycemia or signs of acute complication

If the person cannot safely take carbohydrate, loses consciousness, or has seizures — use prescribed glucagon and call emergency services. Urgent assessment is also needed for vomiting, abdominal pain, rapid breathing, confusion, marked dehydration, or sudden worsening of condition.
