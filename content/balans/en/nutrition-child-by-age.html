NORMAL LIFESTYLE · Nutrition · 0–17 years

Nutrition for children and adolescents by age

Educational material for parents

Most mistakes in child nutrition are not about missing “healthy foods,” but about misjudging the child’s needs: overfeeding milk, restricting fat, replacing meals with juice and purées, fearing allergens, pressuring to finish plates, or putting a teenager on an adult weight-loss diet.

This module answers five practice questions:

  1. How much energy, protein, and fat do children need at different ages?
  2. How does breast milk differ from formula, and when is formula truly needed?
  3. How do you start complementary foods and increase portion size and texture?
  4. How can you tell whether nutrition supports normal growth?
  5. Which common tips are mistakes or myths?

Age-based numbers are a starting point, not a personal prescription. Two healthy children the same age can have very different energy needs because of body size, growth rate, and activity.

E% is the share of total dietary energy. For example, 30 E% fat on a 1600 kcal diet is about 53 g of fat: 1600 × 0.30 ÷ 9.

How to estimate protein

body weight × guideline g/kg = daily protein target

Examples:

  • 2-year-old, 12 kg: 12 × 0.97 ≈ 12 g/day;
  • 8-year-old, 26 kg: 26 × 0.92 ≈ 24 g/day;
  • 15-year-old girl, 55 kg: 55 × 0.85 ≈ 47 g/day;
  • 16-year-old boy, 65 kg: 65 × 0.87 ≈ 57 g/day.

This is a reference for adequate intake, not a ceiling. A typical mixed diet often provides more protein. Extra protein does not automatically become extra height or muscle.

Fat is not the enemy of child nutrition

Fat provides 9 kcal/g, supplies essential fatty acids, and helps absorb vitamins A, D, E, and K. In early childhood it is especially important because energy needs are high relative to stomach size.

6–11 months30–45 E%
12–23 months30–40 E%

Preferred sources: vegetable oils, fish, avocado, nut butters, and ground seeds in a safe form. After age 2, saturated fat should be under 10 E%; trans fats should be minimal.

02 · Growth

Growth: the main sign that nutrition is adequate

How weight gain changes with age

AgeApproximate weight gain
0–3 months150–200 g/week, about 20–30 g/day
3–6 months100–150 g/week, about 15–20 g/day
6–12 months70–90 g/week, about 10–13 g/day
1–2 yearsabout 2–2.5 kg per year
2–5 yearsusually about 1.5–2.5 kg per year
School age before pubertyoften about 2–3 kg per year
Pubertyhighly individual; rate rises markedly during a growth spurt

Classic benchmarks: birth weight usually doubles by about 4–6 months, triples near one year, and is about four times birth weight by age 2. These are not deadlines for every child — they help you see overall trends.

In the first 3 months, growth uses about 35% of total energy; by 12 months about 5%; in the second year less than 2%. That is why lower appetite after the first birthday is often physiological, not a sign of illness.

One number does not tell the story

Growth is assessed from a series of measurements on the appropriate age- and sex-specific chart:

  • under 2 years: weight-for-age, length-for-age, and weight-for-length;
  • after 2 years: height, weight, and BMI-for-age;
  • for children born preterm, use corrected age;
  • the rate and direction of the curve matter, not only the percentile.

A low percentile in a steadily growing child can be a normal variant. Crossing down through several major percentile bands, failure to gain as expected, or weight loss needs evaluation.

When to see a doctor

  • a newborn lost more than 10% of birth weight or did not regain birth weight on time;
  • the child is lethargic, feeds poorly, urinates rarely, or vomits repeatedly;
  • weight has stopped increasing or the curve trends downward;
  • length/height growth has slowed;
  • feeds take unusually long, or there is cough, choking, or pain with feeding;
  • persistent diarrhea, constipation, blood in stool, or marked bloating;
  • a teenager is losing weight quickly, skipping meals, fearing certain foods, or overtraining.

03 · First year

The first year of life

Separate sections cover breast milk, formula, and complementary foods. Content works without JavaScript.

0–6 months — breast milk

0–6 months: breast milk

For a healthy term infant, breast milk is the preferred food for the first 6 months. Its composition changes during a feed and as the baby grows, so it is not a single fixed formula.

Approximate composition of mature breast milk per 100 ml

ComponentAverage content
Energy65–70 kcal
Proteinabout 0.9–1.3 g
Fatabout 3.5–4.5 g
Lactoseabout 6.7–7.8 g

About half of milk energy comes from fat. Beyond macronutrients, breast milk contains immunoglobulins, lactoferrin, oligosaccharides, enzymes, and other bioactive components. Formula supports nutrition and normal growth but does not fully reproduce this biological system.

How often to feed

In the first weeks, many babies nurse 8–12 times per day, sometimes in clusters. Responsive feeding to hunger cues is usually more informative than a rigid schedule. Crying is a late hunger signal.

You cannot reliably estimate intake from feed duration, breast fullness, or pumped volume. Assess effective sucking and swallowing, wet diapers, overall condition, and weight trend.

Common mistakes

  • limiting feeds to fixed 10–15 minutes;
  • giving water or tea instead of feeding a healthy infant without a specific indication;
  • treating frequent evening nursing as proof of “empty milk”;
  • judging milk supply only by pumping output;
  • trying to raise milk fat with a special maternal diet;
  • weighing the baby after every feed daily without a clinical reason.

Myths

“Breast milk becomes empty after a few months.” No. Composition changes, but milk remains a source of energy, nutrients, and bioactive factors.

“Thin or bluish milk is not nutritious.” Appearance does not define energy value.

“You must eat lots of dairy to make milk.” Lactation depends first on effective, regular milk removal; no specific food starts it automatically.

If formula is used

If formula is used

Standard adapted infant formula is a complete alternative when breastfeeding is impossible, insufficient, or not chosen by the family. It is not a reason for guilt or a competition between feeding methods.

Under EU rules, ready-to-feed standard formula provides 60–70 kcal per 100 ml. Protein, fat, carbohydrate, vitamin, and mineral content is regulated; a higher price does not mean better growth or proven benefit.

Safety rules

  • use only the water-to-powder ratio on the label;
  • measure water first, then powder;
  • do not add an extra scoop “for fullness”;
  • do not dilute more for constipation or to save money;
  • do not add cereal to the bottle without a medical indication;
  • prepare and store formula as instructed; keep hands and equipment clean;
  • do not save leftover formula from a started feed for the next time.

Formula that is too concentrated increases osmolar and renal load and can cause dehydration; formula that is too dilute provides insufficient energy and electrolytes.

Types of formula

TypeWhen it is used
Standard cow’s-milk proteinMost healthy term infants
Anti-refluxSelected cases of marked regurgitation after individual assessment
Lactose-freeSome conditions with lactase deficiency; does not treat cow’s-milk protein allergy

Goat’s milk and routine goat-protein formula are not hypoallergenic: goat and cow proteins can cross-react.

Formula myths

“Formula must suit the stool perfectly.” Stool frequency and consistency vary; changing formula with every stool change often disrupts adaptation.

“Hypoallergenic formula prevents any allergy.” Labeling is not a diagnosis, and preventive benefit of a specific product must be supported by evidence.

“Lactose-free formula is needed for milk allergy.” Allergy is usually to protein; lactose is a carbohydrate.

6–11 months — complementary foods

6–11 months: complementary foods

Complementary foods usually start around 6 months when the infant is developmentally ready; they should not begin before 4 months and should generally not be delayed past 6 months without a reason.

Signs of readiness

  • the baby holds the head up and sits steadily with support;
  • reaches for food and opens the mouth when offered;
  • can move food in the mouth and swallow, not only push it out with the tongue;
  • coordinates hands, mouth, and objects.

Teeth are not required.

How much energy complementary foods should provide

For a breastfed infant with average milk intake:

AgeEnergy from complementary foodsFrequency
6–8 monthsabout 200 kcal/day2–3 meals
9–11 monthsabout 300 kcal/day3–4 meals
12–23 monthsabout 550 kcal/day3–4 meals plus 1–2 snacks if needed

That does not mean a portion must be forced. At first, 1–2 teaspoons is enough; volume increases with appetite and skills. By 9–11 months many infants can eat about half a 250 ml cup per meal, but individual variation is wide.

What to offer first

There is no mandatory order of “vegetables → cereal → meat.” From the start, offer nutrient-dense foods regularly, especially iron sources:

  • meat, poultry, fish;
  • egg;
  • legumes and tofu;
  • iron-fortified cereals;
  • plant iron sources paired with vitamin C–rich foods.

Iron need at 7–11 months per NNR 2023 is about 10 mg/day, so complementary feeding based only on fruit and vegetables does not meet the main nutritional goal of this age.

Texture matters as much as composition

  • around 6 months: thick purée, mashed soft food;
  • then: lumps, soft pieces, and safe finger foods;
  • by about 9 months: varied lumpy textures and self-exploration of food;
  • by 12 months: adapted family meals for most children.

Feeding only smooth purées for a long time can delay chewing skills and acceptance of textures.

Allergens

Egg, peanut, fish, wheat, and other potential allergens do not need to be delayed for years. Introduce them after complementary feeding begins, in a safe form, and keep them in the diet if tolerated. With severe eczema, existing allergy, or reaction to a food, plan with a clinician.

Not for routine use in the first year

  • honey before 12 months;
  • unpasteurized products;
  • whole nuts, whole grapes, popcorn, and other high choking-risk foods;
  • whole cow’s or goat’s milk as the main drink before 12 months;
  • juice and sweet drinks;
  • foods with added sugar and excess salt.

Common complementary-feeding mistakes

  • starting with juice or sweet fruit purées;
  • offering only one food for months;
  • making complementary foods too thin: the stomach is full but energy is low;
  • delaying meat, egg, and other iron sources;
  • fearing any lump and staying on purées only;
  • feeding in front of a screen so the child “won’t notice” the amount;
  • forcing a jar or preset portion to be finished;
  • replacing milk with cereal in the bottle;
  • treating refusal after one try as permanent dislike.

Complementary-feeding myths

“Zucchini must be the first food.” There is no universally required first food.

“Allergens should be introduced as late as possible.” Current guidance does not support unnecessary delay; allergens are introduced during complementary feeding in a safe form.

“Salt is absolutely forbidden before one year, even in family food.” You should not add salt for the baby, but the goal is to limit excess — not to fear trace amounts.

“A child without teeth cannot eat pieces.” Soft food can be mashed with gums; shape, softness, and adult supervision matter.

“Baby-led weaning is better than purées for everyone.” Self-feeding can be part of complementary feeding but need not be the only method. Safety, iron, adequate energy, and texture progression matter.

04 · By age

Age-based sections

1–3 years — toddlers

After the first year, growth slows down, so the child may eat noticeably less than parents expect. Appetite can change from day to day. It is more correct to evaluate the diet over several days and growth dynamics, rather than one plate.

Landmarks

  • energy: approximately 750–1050 kcal at 1–2 years and 1000–1400 kcal by 2–3 years;
  • protein: about 1.0 g/kg at 1–2 years and 0.9–1.0 g/kg by 2–3 years;
  • fat: 30–40 E%;
  • regimen: 3 main meals and 2–3 planned snacks;
  • water is the main drink between meals.

Example of the day, not a mandatory menu

EatingExample
BreakfastPorridge with milk/mixture or water, egg or yogurt, soft fruit
SnackNatural yogurt and berries or bread with legume paste
DinnerPotatoes/rice/cereals, meat/fish/legumes, vegetables, a little oil
SnackFruit and cheese or avocado bread
DinnerOmelet/fish/tofu, cereal or bread, vegetables

Portions are determined by appetite. The adult is responsible for available products, time and place; child - for choosing from what is offered and quantity.

Milk after a year

Milk and dairy products are convenient as a source of protein and calcium, but drinking too much milk can crowd out regular food and sources of iron. A practical guideline is usually around 300–500 ml milk/dairy equivalents per day, including yogurt and cheese. Regular volumes well above 500–600 ml require evaluation of the entire diet.

“Baby milk” or 3rd stage formula after one year is not needed by most healthy children with a varied diet; they can be used as one of the products, but do not have a unique mandatory role.

Common mistakes

  • free access to milk, juice and snacks all day;
  • large bottles of milk at night;
  • low-fat foods and eliminating oils for fear of calories;
  • a separate “children’s menu” of pasta, sausages and sweet yoghurt;
  • feeding in pursuit, in front of a screen or just for fun;
  • sweetness as a reward for the main meal;
  • pressure: “three more spoons”, “for mom”, “until you finish eating, you won’t leave.”

Myths

“The child must eat soup every day.” Soup is one of the dish options, and not a physiological necessity. Nutritional value depends on composition and density.

“After a year, you definitely need a special mixture.” For most healthy children, no.

“If a child doesn’t eat meat, he definitely doesn’t have enough protein.” Protein is easily obtained from dairy products, eggs, fish, legumes and soy; the issue of iron is assessed separately.

“I haven’t eaten enough, I need to supplement with cookies.” This reinforces the refusal of staple foods and shifts the diet towards less nutritious calories.

4–6 years

Numbers

  • energy: approximately 1200–1800 kcal/day depending on body size and movement;
  • protein: about 0.85–0.9 g/kg/day;
  • fat: 25–40 E%;
  • dietary fiber: often about 15–20 g/day with appropriate calorie content;
  • calcium: approximately 750–850 mg/day according to age;
  • vitamin D: 10 mcg/day as NNR reference; National supplementation patterns may vary.

Simple main meal design

  • protein source;
  • cereals, potatoes, bread or other source of carbohydrates;
  • vegetables/fruits;
  • source of fat;
  • water.

It is not necessary that the child eats each component in equal quantities. What's more important is repeatable structure and variety throughout the week.

Common mistakes

  • a separate sweet “children’s” menu instead of family food;
  • constant snacking, due to which there is no appetite for meals;
  • juice, compote or drinking yogurt as the main drink;
  • requests to finish an adult portion;
  • the ban on all desserts, creating increased interest in them;
  • discussion of the child’s weight and “fatness” at the table.

Myths

"Sugar causes hyperactivity." No convincing causal relationship with hyperactivity has been demonstrated. Limiting free sugars should be based on the quality of your diet and dental health, not because of the myth of instant behavior change.

“Vitamin gummies will correct a bad diet.” The supplement is not a substitute for nutrition and may lead to excess intake of certain substances.

“A healthy product must be forced to be eaten.” Pressure usually worsens attitudes towards a product. More effective is a regular neutral offer without the requirement to finish eating.

7–10 years

Numbers

  • energy: approximately 1400–2200 kcal/day;
  • protein: about 0.9 g/kg/day;
  • fat: 25–40 E%;
  • dietary fiber: often around 18–28 g/day depending on energy;
  • iron: approximately 8–11 mg/day according to age;
  • calcium: about 800 mg/day.

What's changing

The child spends more time outside the home and chooses some of his food independently. The task of parents is not to control every calorie, but to provide a working system: an affordable breakfast or first meal, school food, a snack before the section and a normal dinner.

School day example

  • at home: porridge/bread + egg/yogurt + fruit;
  • school: full lunch;
  • before the section: banana and yogurt, cheese sandwich or other familiar snack;
  • after the section: a regular dinner with carbohydrates and protein;
  • water is available.

Common mistakes

  • lack of food from the morning until returning from school;
  • money only for sweet pastries and a drink instead of an organized snack;
  • energy drink before training;
  • the diet of the whole family, automatically transferred to the child;
  • health assessment based only on appearance;
  • using food as punishment or reward.

Myths

“Breakfast is a must for everyone and at any cost.” The general diet and performance are important. If the child cannot eat immediately after waking up, the first full meal can be organized later.

“Children should not eat after 18:00.” Meal times vary depending on sleep, school, and workouts. What is more important is the overall diet and the absence of constant uncontrolled snacking.

“Natural juice is equal to fruit.” Juice contains less fiber and releases excess free sugars more easily; whole fruit is preferable.

The rate of maturation is more important than the passport age. A teenager before a growth spurt and a teenager of the same age in the midst of a growth spurt may have very different needs.

Common mistakes

  • skipping meals the entire school day with evening overeating;
  • trying to lose weight by giving up carbohydrates;
  • fear of fats and choosing only low-fat foods;
  • training on an empty stomach after a whole day without food;
  • ignoring heavy menstruation, fatigue and decreased results;
  • uncontrolled iron, vitamin D and sports supplements without indications.

Myths

“Carbohydrates turn into fat in the evening.” The change in mass is determined by the long-term energy balance, and not by the time of one product.

“An overweight teenager just needs to eat less.” Growth, stage of maturation, sleep, medications, family environment, activity, and feeding behavior are assessed. Severe restriction can impair growth and provoke an eating disorder.

“It’s normal for an athlete not to menstruate.” No. This is a possible sign of low energy availability and requires evaluation.

A teenager’s need for sports may exceed the upper limit of the table. If your weight is decreasing, results are deteriorating, you are constantly hungry or recovery is impaired, check your overall energy first rather than adding another protein product.

Common mistakes

  • “drying” and cutting weight for competitions;
  • a diet of protein and vegetables without sufficient carbohydrates and fats;
  • energy drinks instead of sleep and food;
  • replacing breakfast or lunch with a protein bar;
  • taking fat burners, pre-workout supplements and several supplements at the same time;
  • assessment of nutritional normality only by BMI.

Myths

“Protein is hormones or chemistry.” Protein powder is a concentrated food protein. But most teenagers do not need it: the required amount is easier and safer to get with regular food.

“The more protein, the faster the muscles grow.” Without adequate energy, carbohydrates, exercise, and sleep, excess protein provides no benefit.

“For relief, you need to remove fats.” This increases the risk of energy deficiency and hormonal dysfunction.

Young athlete

The most common serious problem for an exercising teenager is not a lack of protein, but a lack of overall energy. Growth, puberty, school and sports compete for the same energy budget.

For a regularly exercising teenager, the protein target is often around 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day, and about 1.5 g/kg/day usually sufficient with adequate energy. It is better to distribute it over 4–5 meals, approximately 0.25–0.3 g/kg at a time.

Example for a 60 kg athlete:

  • daily guideline: approximately 72–96 g of protein;
  • distribution: about 15–20 g in 4–5 divided doses;
  • this is achievable with a regular diet without powder.

Around the workout

WhenWhat do you need
In 1–3 hoursFamiliar foods with carbohydrates, moderate protein, and fluids; less fatty and very voluminous for sensitive gastrointestinal tracts
During a short normal workoutUsually water
During prolonged exercise, heat or heavy sweatingCarbohydrates and electrolytes may be required individually

Examples before training

  • banana + yogurt;
  • bread/lavash with turkey or cheese;
  • porridge with milk;
  • rice or pasta with a small amount of protein.

Examples after training

  • full dinner: potatoes/rice/pasta + meat/fish/legumes + vegetables;
  • yogurt and a sandwich if the main meal is later;
  • milk/kefir and banana as a temporary option, and not a mandatory “anabolic cocktail”.

Signs of Low Energy Availability and RED-S

  • reduction or cessation of growth;
  • puberty disorder;
  • irregular or disappeared menstruation;
  • constant fatigue and cold;
  • decreased results;
  • repeated illnesses and injuries;
  • stress damage to bones;
  • irritability, decreased concentration and sleep;
  • obsessive control of food and weight;
  • decreased libido in older adolescents.

RED-S is possible in girls and boys. A single symptom does not establish a diagnosis, but a combination of signs requires clinical evaluation.
