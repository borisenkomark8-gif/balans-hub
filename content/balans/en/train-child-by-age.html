Physical activity · ages 0–17

Training for children and adolescents by age

A practical framework for building physical activity and training programs for children aged 0–17. Age sets the starting route, but the program is also shaped by movement experience, technique, biological maturation, total training load and health status.

01 · Purpose

Purpose of the module and a quick algorithm

The final decision depends on:

  • movement experience and the quality of fundamental movements;
  • biological maturation and growth rate;
  • goals: health, physical fitness, or a specific sport;
  • total load: school, sports clubs, independent activity, competitions;
  • chronic conditions, medication, injuries and symptoms.
  1. Select the age group.
  2. Clarify clinical restrictions and current symptoms.
  3. Assess training history, technique and weekly load.
  4. Choose the base program: movement → skills → strength → power/specialization.
  5. Set volume and intensity conservatively, then progress one variable at a time.
  6. Monitor tolerance, growth, sleep, pain, motivation and recovery.

02 · General framework

General activity framework

AgeMinimum baseline
Infants under 1 yearActivity several times a day; for infants who are not yet crawling or walking, at least 30 minutes of tummy time in total across waking periods
1–2 yearsAt least 180 minutes of varied physical activity of any intensity, spread throughout the day
3–4 yearsAt least 180 minutes of activity, including at least 60 minutes of moderate- or vigorous-intensity activity
5–17 yearsAn average of at least 60 minutes/day of moderate- or vigorous-intensity activity; vigorous aerobic, muscle-strengthening and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days/week
Important. This is a target for total daily movement, not the duration of a single organized training session.

03 · Intensity

Intensity scale

RPE (Rating of Perceived Exertion) is a subjective rating of exercise intensity on a scale of 0 to 10, where 0 means no effort and 10 means maximal effort.

LevelSignsPractical marker
LightBreathing barely changes; the child can speak freelyRPE 2–3/10
ModerateBreathing is faster; the child speaks in sentencesRPE 4–5/10
VigorousMarkedly faster breathing; the child speaks in short phrasesRPE 6–8/10
MaximalSpeech is difficult; technique deteriorates quicklyRPE 9–10/10; usually not required in health-oriented programs

For strength work, the main regulators are maintaining technique and RIR (Repetitions in Reserve — repetitions left before failure). Base range: finish each set with 2–4 good-quality repetitions in reserve. Heart rate varies widely in children and should not be the only criterion of intensity.

04 · By age

Age-based programs

Under 3 years — physical activity, not training

Priorities

  • free movement on the floor and safe exploration of the environment;
  • rolling, crawling, pulling up to stand, walking, carrying light objects;
  • running, climbing, throwing, catching large balls, balance;
  • minimizing prolonged restraint in a stroller, high chair or car seat when it is not necessary.

Format

  • short episodes of 3–10 minutes, repeated many times throughout the day;
  • play with an adult, without a formal plan of sets and repetitions;
  • difficulty increases by changing the environment and the task, not by adding external load.

Avoid

  • forced teaching of a motor skill the child is not ready for;
  • prolonged monotonous activity;
  • competition, or performing activity through fear, pain or marked fatigue.
3–5 years — fundamental movements

Goals

  • running, stopping and changing direction;
  • jumping and landing safely;
  • throwing, catching and kicking a ball;
  • balance, rhythm and spatial orientation;
  • a positive association between movement and play.

Organized session

20–35 minutes, 2–4 times/week, as part of overall daily movement.

  1. Active game — 5 minutes.
  2. Two or three movement skills — 10–15 minutes.
  3. A simple obstacle course or relay — 5–10 minutes.
  4. Calm cool-down — 3–5 minutes.

Strength component

  • squatting to a support, getting up from the floor, carrying, pushing, resistance-band pulling, supported hanging;
  • 1–2 sets of 6–12 good-quality repetitions, or playful intervals of 10–20 seconds;
  • light objects; the aim is body control, not fatigue.

Progression

Accuracy of movement comes first, followed by more variations and slightly longer duration. Do not turn the session into a scaled-down copy of an adult workout.

6–9 years — physical literacy

Goals

  • a solid repertoire of fundamental movement skills;
  • bilateral coordination and control of body position;
  • learning the main strength movement patterns;
  • developing speed through short, playful accelerations;
  • exposure to a variety of activities without early narrow specialization.

Week

  • daily movement in line with the general guideline;
  • 2 strength sessions of 25–40 minutes;
  • 2–4 blocks of speed, coordination or jumping work of 10–20 minutes, usually within a game or training session;
  • aerobic work that is mostly playful and interval-based, following the child's natural rhythm.

Strength work

ParameterStarting range
ExercisesSquat, hinge/pull, lunge, push, pull, carry, stabilization, climbing
Volume1–2 sets × 6–12 repetitions
IntensityRPE 4–6/10; RIR 3–4
Rest60–120 seconds, or until technique has recovered
Frequency2 times/week, preferably on non-consecutive days

Resistance is acceptable under qualified supervision. The criterion for starting is the ability to follow instructions and repeat the movement with consistent technique, not reaching a particular age.

Speed and jumping

  • accelerations of 5–15 m, 4–8 repetitions with full recovery;
  • low-volume jumps and landings: 2–3 exercises × 3–5 repetitions;
  • stop the series when speed drops or body position deteriorates.
10–12 years — technique and structure

Goals

  • consolidating technique in the main strength movements;
  • developing relative strength, coordination and speed;
  • learning to warm up, self-monitor and keep a training log;
  • maintaining variety in activity as sport specialization begins.

Week

  • 2–3 strength sessions of 35–55 minutes;
  • 2 speed and coordination blocks;
  • aerobic work: games, continuous activity or moderate/vigorous intervals, taking into account the total volume of sport;
  • at least one day without organized vigorous activity.

Strength work

ParameterBase range
Volume2–3 sets × 6–12 repetitions
Intensitymostly RPE 5–7/10; RIR 2–4
Exercises5–8 whole-body movements
Rest1–3 minutes depending on exercise complexity
Progression+1–2 repetitions, then the smallest possible increase in resistance while maintaining technique

Periods of rapid growth call for a temporary reduction in difficulty and re-tuning of technique. Pain around the apophyses and a persistent decline in coordination are reasons to adjust the volume of jumping, running and sport-specific load.

Testing

  • technique in squatting, hinging, pushing, pulling and landing;
  • standing long jump, medicine-ball throw, short sprint;
  • a submaximal running test when needed;
  • strength tests — mostly submaximal. 1RM (one-repetition maximum) is acceptable only in technically proficient children, using a validated protocol and under qualified supervision; it is not required for routine health-oriented programming.
13–15 years — load management during maturation

Goals

  • developing strength and power without loss of movement quality;
  • managing abrupt changes in height, body mass and limb leverage;
  • combining general conditioning with sport specialization;
  • teaching recovery and recognition of the signs of overload.

Week

  • 2–3 strength sessions of 45–70 minutes;
  • 1–2 targeted speed/power blocks, if the child is recovered;
  • aerobic load is distributed around sports clubs and competitions;
  • separate hard days with light or recovery days.

Strength work

GoalVolume and intensity
Learning/correcting technique2–3 × 8–12; RPE 4–6
General strength2–4 × 5–10; RPE 6–8; RIR 2–3
Power2–4 × 3–6; light/moderate resistance; maximal quality and speed
Local muscular endurance2–3 × 10–15; RPE 5–7

Heavy sets are introduced only after technique is firmly established. Repetitions to failure, forced repetitions and frequent maximal attempts are not a basic tool.

Monitoring at this age

  • morning well-being, sleep, muscle soreness, motivation;
  • trends in pain and performance;
  • height and weight — for context, without daily monitoring;
  • signs of low energy availability and RED-S (see the "Load monitoring" section).
16–17 years — transition to the adult model

Goals

  • an individualized program for health or a sport-specific goal;
  • self-regulation of training load;
  • developing strength, power, aerobic capacity and sport-specific endurance;
  • preparing to continue training safely into adulthood.

Base model

  • 2–4 strength sessions/week, depending on training history and sport;
  • train each major muscle group twice a week, or provide an equivalent weekly volume;
  • combine light, moderate and heavy days;
  • account for high-intensity aerobic and speed work together with game and competition load.

Strength work

LevelApproach
Beginner2–3 × 6–12; RPE 5–7; technique and consistency
Trained3–5 × 3–10; RPE 6–8; periodization according to the goal
Sport specializationindividualized strength/power blocks; monitoring of total load and competition calendar

Age alone does not make an adolescent ready for adult training volumes. The program progresses according to actual training history.

05 · Session structure

Universal structure of a strength session

  1. Readiness check: symptoms, pain, sleep, recovery — 1–2 minutes.
  2. Warm-up: general activity + dynamic mobility — 5–10 minutes.
  3. Speed/coordination/jumps: while the child is not yet fatigued — 5–15 minutes.
  4. Main movements: squat, hinge, push, pull — 15–30 minutes.
  5. Unilateral work, carries, core: 5–15 minutes.
  6. Cool-down and logging the session: 3–5 minutes.

Progression rules

  • increase one variable at a time: repetitions, sets, resistance, difficulty or frequency;
  • stable technique across all planned repetitions comes first;
  • as a guide, increase resistance by the smallest available step, usually no more than 5–10%;
  • after illness, injury, school holidays or a period of rapid growth, step back by one or two levels;
  • when overall sport load rises noticeably, do not automatically increase strength volume;
  • consider a deload every 4–8 weeks based on condition and calendar rather than a rigid template.

06 · Load monitoring

Load monitoring

sRPE (session Rating of Perceived Exertion) is a subjective rating of how hard the whole session was on a 0–10 scale, usually given 15–30 minutes after it ends.

Session load = sRPE × session duration in minutes. Example: 60 minutes × sRPE 6 = 360 arbitrary units.

RED-S (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) is a syndrome of relative energy deficiency in sport: poorer recovery and performance, persistent fatigue, recurrent injuries or stress fractures, and disturbances of growth and puberty. RED-S can occur in both girls and boys; menstrual disturbance is one possible sign in girls but not the only criterion.

07 · Safety

Medical assessment and stopping activity

When medical assessment is needed before starting or changing a program

  • chest pain/pressure, unexplained fainting or near-fainting during exercise;
  • palpitations with dizziness, pain or reduced performance;
  • disproportionate shortness of breath, cyanosis, falling oxygen saturation;
  • known heart disease, uncontrolled arterial hypertension;
  • poorly controlled asthma or frequent exercise-related symptoms;
  • diabetes with recurrent severe hypoglycemia, ketosis or no management plan;
  • acute illness, fever, or a history of myocarditis/pericarditis without clearance;
  • progressive neurological symptoms;
  • pain that changes gait or technique, night pain, increasing swelling;
  • signs of an eating disorder or RED-S.

Stop activity immediately

  • chest pain, fainting, confusion;
  • sudden marked shortness of breath, or wheezing that does not respond to the agreed plan;
  • signs of severe hypoglycemia;
  • acute neurological deficit;
  • sudden sharp localized pain, deformity or inability to bear weight;
  • any rapidly worsening symptoms.

Minimum data set for personalization

  • age, height, weight and growth trajectory;
  • pubertal stage — only when clinically justified and with respect for privacy;
  • goal and preferred types of activity;
  • training/competitions over the past 4 weeks;
  • training history and movements already mastered;
  • diagnoses, symptoms, injuries, medications and medical restrictions;
  • sleep, school workload, perceived recovery;
  • available equipment and the qualifications of the supervising adult.
